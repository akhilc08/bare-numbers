# Grounding, common ground, clarification-asking, and the cost of asking in LLMs and LLM agents (related work for L4, "Bare Numbers Between Agents")

Scope note: these notes are current as of 2026-10-07. Every arXiv ID below was checked against the arXiv API (export.arxiv.org) in this session, including title, authors, date, and the author-supplied comment/journal-ref fields. Venues come from the arXiv comment field, the ACL Anthology page, or Semantic Scholar (S2) as stated. Anything I could not confirm is marked UNVERIFIED. Each paper entry gives: citation, ID/URL, finding, **Relation** to L4 (supports / novelty threat / contrast / borrowable method), and **Pos.** (a one-sentence positioning statement L4 could use).

L4 setup in brief: a user's sender agent relays a bare number ("budget 300,000") to a receiver agent at another organisation whose tool uses a different unit. The receiver either acts (correctly or silently wrong) or asks. The paper currently scores "safe = correct or asked". In the hybrid channel the sender attaches a confidence and the receiver asks when the unit is missing or the confidence is low. With Opus this gives 66% correct and 1% silently wrong, against 85% correct and 7% silently wrong without it.

## (a) Grounding and presumptive grounding in LLMs: what Shaikh et al. found, and what followed (incl. agent–agent grounding)

### Takeaway
Shaikh et al. (NAACL 2024) showed that LLMs "presume common ground": they produce far fewer clarifications, acknowledgments, and follow-ups than humans, and preference optimization (DPO) makes this worse. Follow-ups through 2026 confirm the gap in real logs and show that early grounding failures predict breakdowns. Grounding is still studied almost entirely as human–LLM. I found no paper that measures grounding acts between two LLM agents that represent different principals, which is L4's setting.

### Cited Findings
- **Shaikh, Gligorić, Khetan, Gerstgrasser, Yang, Jurafsky. "Grounding Gaps in Language Model Generations." NAACL 2024 (Long), pp. 6279–6296, Anthology 2024.naacl-long.348; arXiv:2311.09144.** The v1 title was "Grounding or Guesswork? Large Language Models are Presumptive Grounders", which is where the "presumptive grounding" term comes from. — [arXiv v1/v2 abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09144); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.348/)
  - The paper curates three grounding acts (clarification, acknowledgment, follow-up) and simulates turn-taking on ESConv, TSCC, and Persuasion-for-Good. Its finding: "compared to humans — LLMs generate language with less conversational grounding, instead generating text that appears to simply presume common ground." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09144)
  - Reported gaps include 64.3% fewer follow-ups and 83.4% fewer acknowledgments than humans, with a clarification rate of 0.0 for ChatGPT-3.5 on some datasets. LLM–human agreement on clarification is near zero. — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.09144v2)
  - SFT shows no meaningful correlation with grounding. More DPO training on Mistral-7B degrades agreement on all grounding acts (R = −0.70 to −0.89, p < 0.05). In the preference data, preferred responses contain significantly fewer questions (χ² = 484.08). A mitigation prompt raises the follow-up base rate from 10.76% to 42.32%, but clarification agreement does not improve. — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.09144v2)
  - **Relation:** supports. It is the canonical citation for the claim that LLM receivers presume rather than establish common ground. In L4 the receiver presumes the sender's unit matches its own tool's unit. **Pos.:** "L4 extends presumptive grounding from human–LLM dialogue to agent–agent relays, where the presumed common ground is a unit convention that neither party mentions."
- **Shaikh, Mozannar, Bansal, Fourney, Horvitz. "Navigating Rifts in Human-LLM Grounding: Study and Benchmark." ACL 2025; arXiv:2503.13975.** Using WildChat, MultiWOZ, and Bing Chat logs, LLMs were "three times less likely to initiate clarification and sixteen times less likely to provide follow-up requests than humans", and "early grounding failures predict later interaction breakdowns." The paper releases the Rifts benchmark, on which frontier models do poorly. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13975)
  - **Relation:** supports. It is also a borrowable method: forecasting grounding failure from early turns. **Pos.:** "Rifts measures missed grounding in human–LLM logs. L4 measures a related failure in an agent–agent relay where silently wrong actions are observable."
- **Mohapatra et al. "Evaluating the Effectiveness of Large Language Models in Establishing Conversational Grounding." EMNLP 2024 (main), Anthology 2024.emnlp-main.545.** The search-surfaced summary reports that grounding ability scales with pre-training data size, that models under 7B perform near chance, and that "cancellation" (explicit retraction) is hard: GPT-3.5 picks the correct grounded response only 55% of the time. The full author list and numbers were not opened in this session, so they are UNVERIFIED beyond the Anthology listing. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.545/)
  - **Relation:** contrast/background. It evaluates grounding as comprehension (choosing a grounded response), not as initiating repair. **Pos.:** "Prior grounding evaluations test whether models understand grounded content. L4 tests whether an agent initiates grounding when a convention may differ."
- **Elizabeth, Lecorvé, Rojas-Barahona, Ochs. "Conversational Grounding in Large Language Models: Evaluation Methods, Challenges and Future Directions." SIGDIAL 2026, pp. 151–163.** This survey covers grounding evaluation in task-oriented dialogue. It reviews explicit grounding (dialogue acts, participant mental-state modelling) and implicit, outcome-level evaluation in collaborative tasks, and it criticizes current metrics. It states that "LLMs make very little effort in clarifying ambiguities", that failure to ground "can lead agents to provide confident responses based on incomplete information", and it proposes that systems model the listener ("adapt to the listener's expertise to decide … how much it needs to be grounded"). I read the PDF text: the survey is framed around human–machine dialogue and has no agent–agent section. A WebFetch summary had claimed it covers agent–agent grounding, and that claim is wrong. — [ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2026.sigdial-1.11.pdf)
  - **Relation:** supports. It is the up-to-date survey to cite, and it confirms an agent–agent gap. **Pos.:** "A 2026 survey of grounding evaluation focuses on human–machine task-oriented dialogue. L4 supplies an outcome-level grounding test between agents."
- **Cemri et al. "Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?" arXiv:2503.13657 (v3); S2 lists NeurIPS 2025.** MAST is built from more than 1,600 traces across 7 MAS frameworks, with 14 failure modes in 3 categories (κ = 0.88). The inter-agent misalignment category includes **FM-2.2 "Fail to ask for clarification"** ("inability to request additional information when faced with unclear or incomplete data"), at 6.80% prevalence in v3, plus information withholding (0.85%) and ignored other agent's input (1.90%). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657); [HTML v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3)
  - **Relation:** supports. This is the strongest existing evidence that missing clarification between LLM agents is a real failure mode. **Pos.:** "MAST lists failure to ask for clarification as an inter-agent failure mode in multi-agent traces. L4 isolates one cause, a unit convention mismatch, and measures it in a controlled way."
- **Wang et al. "GroundAct: Can LLM Agents Ground Actions in Environmental States?" arXiv:2508.05614.** Agents succeed 85–96% when the instruction fully specifies the action, but only 29–53% when feasibility depends on unmentioned environment state. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.05614)
  - **Relation:** contrast. This is "grounding" in the environmental sense, not the conversational one. **Pos.:** "L4's missing information is a convention held by another agent, not state the receiver could observe."
- **Jiang, Qian, Wang, Lu, Ji, Peng. "ACCORD: Action-Conditioned Contextual Grounding for Language Agents." arXiv:2606.16432.** Agents "act from assumed rather than observed specifics". Probing the environment before each action improves AppWorld task-goal completion from 42.0% to 62.6% with GPT-5-mini, +10.8 with Claude-4.5-sonnet. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16432)
  - **Relation:** contrast, with a borrowable baseline (a receiver that probes its own tool schema before acting). **Pos.:** "ACCORD fixes assumptions by probing the environment. In L4 the receiver's environment is consistent and the ambiguity sits in the other agent's convention, which only communication can resolve."

### Inferences
- The grounding literature gives L4 its framing: the receiver presumes common ground. It also gives L4 a mechanism: preference tuning disfavors questions. L4 extends this from humans to agents, and this extension is the main gap the literature leaves open.
- MAST's FM-2.2 shows that clarification failures between agents happen in the wild. No study controls for which shared convention was missing, and L4 does.

### Gaps
- I found no study that counts grounding acts (clarification/acknowledgment) in LLM-to-LLM dialogue across organisational boundaries, with each agent representing a different principal.
- Mohapatra et al. numbers come from a search summary. The PDF was not opened.

## (b) The four 2026 arXiv papers cited by Review 3: verified titles, methods, and closeness to the hybrid protocol

### Takeaway
All four IDs exist and the titles match clarification/underspecification work in agents. **2606.19559 is the closest:** it elicits a separate request-uncertainty score (0/0.5/1) and *forces a clarification when u ≥ 0.5*. That is close to the hybrid's "ask when confidence is low". 2603.26233 decouples underspecification detection into a separate agent, but uses a binary decision with no numeric confidence. 2606.03135 (an RL-trained clarifier with an information-gain reward) and 2605.07937 (the timing of clarification) are further away. **None of the four transmits a confidence from a different agent, and all four score asking as free or nearly free.** That shared weakness is the same one reviewers raised against L4.

### Cited Findings
- **Matsnev, G. "Uncertainty Decomposition for Clarification Seeking in LLM Agents." arXiv:2606.19559 (June 2026, no venue).** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19559); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19559)
  - Method: a prompt-based decomposition that separates action confidence c_t from request uncertainty u_t. The agent rates u on an anchored 3-point scale. 0.0 means the "goal fully specifies every parameter"; 0.5 means the goal leaves open a choice with a likely unstated preference; 1.0 means "critical details are missing". The rule is "If u_request ≥ θ, your action MUST be request_clarification", with θ = 0.5 by default and a sensitivity analysis at 0.25 and 0.75.
  - Evaluation: WebShop-Clarification and ALFWorld-Clarification, each with 50% of tasks deliberately underspecified, across five backbones (GPT-5.1, DeepSeek-v3.2-exp, GLM-4.7, Qwen3.5-35B, GPT-OSS-120B). Clarification is scored as binary classification (precision/recall/F1) against underspecification labels. Clarification F1 on ALFWorld-Clarification improves 73% over ReAct+UE and 36% over UAM. When the agent asks, "the original fully specified goal is revealed", so the simulated user is an oracle. **Asking carries no penalty.** Adding the u-assessment cost 0.8 pp of task success. All methods were overconfident.
  - **Relation: novelty threat (moderate–high) and borrowable method.** "Ask when a self-rated ambiguity score crosses a threshold" is nearly the hybrid's receiver rule. Differences: (i) in L4 the confidence is produced by the *sender* and crosses an agent boundary, while in 2606.19559 it is self-assessed and internal. (ii) L4's ambiguity is a *convention mismatch* that a receiver cannot see from the request text, while 2606.19559's tasks are visibly underspecified. (iii) L4 can measure silent-wrong rates, while 2606.19559 measures clarification F1. **Pos.:** "Unlike threshold-triggered self-clarification (Matsnev 2026), the hybrid moves the uncertainty estimate to the party that holds the convention and sends it across the inter-agent channel."
- **Edwards, N., Schuster, S. "Ask or Assume? Uncertainty-Aware Clarification-Seeking in Coding Agents." arXiv:2603.26233 (v3: "EMNLP 2026 camera-ready").** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26233); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26233)
  - Method: an underspecified variant of SWE-bench Verified. A two-agent scaffold pairs a Main Agent (coding tools) with an Intent Agent that reads the history and outputs a structured JSON decision on whether clarification is needed. It uses no numeric confidence or threshold ("a formalized measure of confidence" is explicitly avoided). GPT-5.1 simulates the user from the fully specified issue, capped at 3 interaction turns.
  - Results: 69.40% resolve rate (UA-Multi), against fully specified baselines of about 70.8% (Claude) and 74.6% (Kimi). The no-interaction "Hidden" setting reaches 54.8% (Claude). Ask rates were 62–72% of tasks for Claude, averaging 3.06 queries per questioned task, and 83–100% for Kimi, averaging 8.71. When the Claude scaffold asked, resolve rate was 65.99%, and 76.92% when it did not. **No scoring penalty for asking**; the metric is overall resolve rate.
  - **Relation: novelty threat (moderate).** It shares the decoupling of "detect underspecification" from "execute", and the multi-agent framing. But the two agents serve one principal, and the "multi-agent" part is an internal scaffold, not inter-organisational relay. Its very high ask rates under free asking support the reviewers' point that free asking inflates safety. **Pos.:** "Edwards & Schuster decouple ambiguity detection inside one agent system serving one user. L4 studies two agents serving different principals, where the receiver cannot see the sender's convention."
- **Deng, Li, Li, Zhu, Zhao, Guo, Wang. "Uncertainty-Aware Clarification in LLM Agents with Information Gain." arXiv:2606.03135; journal-ref ICML 2026.** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03135); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.03135)
  - Method: a "clarification-enhanced τ-Bench" with deliberately vague instructions. A clarifier LLM is trained (DAPO) with an **Information Gain Reward**: the normalized log-likelihood gain of the ground-truth goal after the clarification Q/A, which equals the PMI between answer and goal. The user simulator has a relaxed mode (evaluation) and a **strict** mode (training) that "reveals task-specific information only when the question is specific and relevant", to stop generic questions from being rewarded. There is no confidence-threshold rule; when to ask is learned.
  - Results: +3.7% success over no-clarification while adding only 0.3 interaction steps on average. The learned clarifier uses 1.3 clarification turns per task, against 3.9–5.1 for general-purpose models. Retail/Airline average: 15.4% with no clarification, 17.8% for this method, 18% for the full-intent oracle.
  - **Relation: borrowable method (strict simulator, information-gain scoring, steps reporting); low novelty threat.** It is a single agent and a single user, with no transmitted confidence. **Pos.:** "Information-gain clarifiers optimise which question a single agent asks. L4 asks what the sender should put in the message so the receiver knows whether to ask at all."
- **Gulati, Gupta, Lumer, Sen, Subbiah. "Ask Early, Ask Late, Ask Right: When Does Clarification Timing Matter for Long-Horizon Agents?" arXiv:2605.07937 (May 2026).** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07937); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07937)
  - Method: "forced injection" of ground-truth clarifications at 10/30/50/70/90% of the trajectory, across four information dimensions (goal, input, constraint, context). It uses MCP-Atlas, TheAgentCompany, and SWE-Bench Pro with GPT-5.2, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 3 Flash, and DeepSeek V3.2: 84 task variants and more than 6,000 runs. Metrics: pass@3 and "wasted compute" (pre-injection actions not in oracle traces). The method has no explicit asking cost.
  - Results: goal clarification loses nearly all value after 10% of execution (pass@3 drops from 0.78 to baseline). Input clarification keeps value to about 50%. "Deferring any clarification type past mid-trajectory degrades performance below never asking at all." In 300 unscripted sessions, "no current frontier model asks within the empirically optimal window". GPT-5.2 asks in 52% of sessions yet succeeds less (3%) than Claude (23% asking, 11% success), and Gemini never asks. The paper grounds itself in value-of-information theory (Howard 1966).
  - **Relation: contrast plus a borrowable metric (timing, wasted compute).** L4's unit is an *input*-type dimension, which the paper finds tolerates later asking. **Pos.:** "Timing studies inject oracle answers into single-agent trajectories. L4 studies whether the receiver ever learns it should ask, before its one tool call."

### Inferences
- Review 3's resemblance claim holds for the *receiver policy* (an ask-on-low-uncertainty threshold, as in Matsnev 2606.19559) and for the *decoupled detector* (Edwards & Schuster 2603.26233). It does not hold for the *channel design*: none of the four has the sender attach calibrated confidence for the receiver to use. L4 should concede the policy resemblance and claim the channel and the convention-mismatch diagnosis.
- All four score asking as free or nearly free. In 2606.19559 the oracle reveals the goal at zero cost. In 2603.26233 the only limit is a 3-turn cap. 2606.03135 reports steps but optimises success. 2605.07937 has no asking cost. L4 can therefore turn the reviewer objection into a contribution: a cost-weighted evaluation, which these concurrent papers lack.
- ARR timing (UNVERIFIED policy detail): ACL/ARR treats papers made public within about 3 months of the submission deadline as contemporaneous. All four Review-3 papers are older than that. The latest, 2606.19559 (June 17, 2026), is about 4 months before an October 2026 deadline, so L4 should discuss all four as prior work. Under the same rule, ClarifyCodeBench (Jul 1), RegretBench (Jul 23), CIGAsk (Sep 21), and Pareto-Dominant Clarification (Oct 2) are borderline or contemporaneous.

### Gaps
- I did not read 2606.19559's results tables per backbone, or 2603.26233's per-model ask rates on fully specified tasks. Both would show how much over-asking happens on well-specified inputs.

## (c) Clarification benchmarks and methods (when to ask, ambiguity detection, underspecification): which simulate an answering user, and how they score turns and cost

### Takeaway
Most clarification benchmarks now simulate an answering user, usually an LLM given privileged ground truth. Costs are scored in five ways: (1) not at all (success only), (2) counts of turns, questions, or redundant questions, (3) turn-discounted gain (nDCG-style), (4) deviation from an optimal number of rounds, or (5) cost-weighted success or regret against a reference policy. Benchmarks that include *well-specified* items (to catch over-asking) are the minority. Benchmarks where the ambiguity is a units or precision convention exist only in code generation (ClarifyCodeBench), where those types are among the *easiest* to resolve once asked.

### Cited Findings
**Benchmarks**
- **Min, Michael, Hajishirzi, Zettlemoyer. "AmbigQA: Answering Ambiguous Open-domain Questions." EMNLP 2020; arXiv:2004.10645.** AmbigNQ has 14,042 questions, and "over half of the questions in NQ-open are ambiguous". The task is to produce all plausible answers plus disambiguated rewrites. There is no user simulator. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.10645)
  - **Relation:** contrast. Static, single-agent QA ambiguity. **Pos.:** "Unlike AmbigQA's surface-visible referential ambiguity, L4's ambiguity cannot be seen in the request."
- **Zhang, Qin, Deng, Huang, Lei, Liu, Jin, Liang, Chua. "CLAMBER: A Benchmark of Identifying and Clarifying Ambiguous Information Needs in Large Language Models." ACL 2024; arXiv:2405.12063.** About 12K items with a taxonomy of ambiguity. LLMs have "limited practical utility … in identifying and clarifying ambiguous user queries". CoT and few-shot prompting "may result in overconfidence" with only marginal gains. Evaluation is static, with no answering user. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12063)
  - **Relation:** supports (overconfidence under prompting). **Pos.:** "CLAMBER shows that prompting does not reliably produce ambiguity detection. L4 tests whether a channel-level signal does."
- **Li, Wu, Chang. "ClarEval: A Benchmark for Evaluating Clarification Skills of Code Agents under Ambiguous Instructions." arXiv:2603.00187 (Feb 2026).** Injects three ambiguity types (missing goals, missing premises, ambiguous terminology) into coding tasks. Metrics are **Average Turns to Clarify (ATC)** and **Key Question Coverage (KQC)**, reported alongside code correctness. Eleven agents were evaluated. Strong coders such as "GPT-5-Coder … often lack the strategic communication skills required". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00187)
  - **Relation:** borrowable metric (ATC). **Pos.:** "ClarEval scores clarification efficiency between a code agent and a user. L4 applies turn-aware scoring between two agents."
- **Fang, Jin, Dong, Li, Zhang, Jin, Li. "ClarifyCodeBench: Evaluating LLMs on Clarifying Ambiguous Requirements for Code Generation." arXiv:2607.00711 (July 2026).** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00711); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.00711)
  - Ten ambiguity types, **including "Units" ("quantities without clear unit specifications") and "Numerical Precision"**. The claim to check holds: "DeepSeek-V3.2, Gemini-2.5-Flash, and GPT-4o each reach 66.7% on Units, and the same three models each reach 100.0% on Numerical Precision", which makes these the best-resolved types. Collection Semantics stays at or below 16.7% for every model.
  - User and judge: GPT-4o matches generated questions to annotated key questions and supplies the ground-truth answers. Metrics: **Turn-discounted Key Question Rate (TKQR)**, an nDCG-style reward for finding key questions early, and **Optimal Round Adherence (ORA)**, a Gaussian penalty around the optimal number of rounds (ORA = 0.5 when |n−K| = 0.5K). Best TKQR is 0.30 (DeepSeek-V3.2). Thinking models gain little (GPT-5 TKQR 0.21). Pass@1 drops sharply under ambiguity.
  - **Relation: contrast (important).** When the unit gap is *visible* in a single-agent spec, models resolve it relatively well. L4's point is that in a relay the receiver does not see that a unit is in question, because the bare number looks fully specified. The 66.7% figure rests on very few items, given the small per-type n implied by 66.7%/100%, so treat it as indicative. **Pos.:** "Unit ambiguity is among the easiest types to clarify when it is visible in a requirement (ClarifyCodeBench). L4 shows it becomes a silent failure when it is hidden behind a relayed bare number."
- **Sirdeshmukh, Wetter. "Implicit Intelligence — Evaluating Agents on What Users Don't Say." arXiv:2602.20424 (Feb 2026).** 205 scenarios testing implicit requirements (accessibility, privacy, catastrophic risk, contextual constraints) in an "Agent-as-a-World" harness that simulates YAML-defined worlds with LLMs. Constraints are "discoverable through environmental exploration". Across 16 models, the best scenario pass rate is 48.3%. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20424)
  - **Relation:** supports (underspecification is the norm) and contrast (constraints are discovered from the environment, not through questions to the user or another agent). **Pos.:** "Implicit Intelligence tests unstated constraints a single agent can discover. L4 tests unstated conventions only the other agent knows."
- **Yao, Shinn, Razavi, Narasimhan. "τ-bench: A Benchmark for Tool-Agent-User Interaction in Real-World Domains." arXiv:2406.12045 (venue on arXiv: none; ICLR 2025 is UNVERIFIED).** The user is simulated by an LM. Scoring compares the final database state against the annotated goal state, and the paper introduces **pass^k** reliability (gpt-4o succeeds on less than 50% of tasks; pass^8 is below 25% in retail). Asking is not costed. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045)
  - **Relation:** borrowable method (LM user simulator, state-based scoring, pass^k). **Pos.:** "L4 adopts τ-bench-style simulated principals and outcome checks, and adds an explicit price per question."
- **Barres, Dong, Ray, Si, Narasimhan. "τ²-Bench: Evaluating Conversational Agents in a Dual-Control Environment." arXiv:2506.07982.** Modeled as a Dec-POMDP in which both the agent and the user act with tools. The user simulator is "tightly coupled with the environment". It separates reasoning errors from communication/coordination errors. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07982)
  - **Relation:** borrowable method (decomposing communication errors from reasoning errors). **Pos.:** "τ² separates communication errors from reasoning errors for agent–user pairs. L4 does the same for agent–agent relays."
- **Ross, Mahabaleshwarkar, Suhara. "When2Call: When (not) to Call Tools." NAACL 2025; arXiv:2504.18851.** A multiple-choice benchmark of tool-calling decisions: when to call, *when to ask follow-up questions*, and when to admit the tools cannot answer. SOTA tool-callers leave "significant room for improvement". Preference-optimization training helps. No user simulator. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18851)
  - **Relation:** borrowable (a decision-only evaluation with ask as an option). **Pos.:** "When2Call scores the ask/act/refuse choice statically. L4 scores it end-to-end against a simulated principal."
- **Wang et al. "Learning to Ask: When LLM Agents Meet Unclear Instruction" (NoisyToolBench, Ask-when-Needed). EMNLP 2025; arXiv:2409.00557.** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00557); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.00557v4)
  - "LLMs tend to arbitrarily generate the missed argument." Error categories in real user instructions: missing key information 56.0%, multiple references 11.3%, **instructions with errors 17.3%**, beyond tool capabilities 15.3%.
  - **ToolEvaluator** is an automated user. It answers *only* if the question's sentence-embedding similarity to an expected question passes a threshold, and otherwise replies "Sorry, I cannot provide additional information about this." Metrics: A1 (asked the expected question), A2 (correct API and arguments), A3 (task completion), **Re (redundant questions)**, and **Steps**. ToolEvaluator agreed with human annotation 90% of the time on 50 cases.
  - **Relation:** supports (agents fabricate missing tool arguments, which matches L4's silent wrong unit) and borrowable (gated simulator, redundant-question and steps metrics). **Pos.:** "NoisyToolBench shows that tool agents fill missing arguments with guesses. L4 shows that a filled argument (the number) can be present and still be wrong in units."
- **Zhang, Deng, Ren, Ng, Chua. "Ask-before-Plan: Proactive Language Agents for Real-World Planning." EMNLP 2024 Findings; arXiv:2406.12639.** Introduces the Proactive Agent Planning task (predict clarification needs, gather information with tools, plan) and the Clarification-Execution-Planning (CEP) multi-agent framework. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12639)
  - **Relation:** novelty threat (low). It also uses a dedicated clarification agent, but for one user. **Pos.:** "CEP specialises one agent for clarification inside one system. L4's clarification crosses organisations."
- **Vijayvargiya, Zhou, Yerukola, Sap, Neubig. "Ambig-SWE: Interactive Agents to Overcome Underspecificity in Software Engineering." ICLR 2026; arXiv:2502.13069.** Evaluates three steps separately: detecting underspecification, asking targeted questions, and using the answers. "Models struggle to distinguish between well-specified and underspecified instructions", yet interaction gives improvements of up to 74% over non-interactive settings. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13069)
  - **Relation:** borrowable (the three-step decomposition and a well-specified control). **Pos.:** "Following Ambig-SWE, L4 separately reports detection (did the receiver ask when it should), question quality, and use of the answer."
- **Qian et al. "Tell Me More! Towards Implicit User Intention Understanding of Language Model Driven Agents" (IN3, Mistral-Interact). arXiv:2402.09205; S2 lists ACL 2024.** An upstream model judges task vagueness, asks about intentions, and summarizes the missing details before execution. It reduces redundant tool use. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09205)
  - **Relation:** contrast. Human-to-agent. **Pos.:** "IN3 inserts a clarifier between user and agent. L4's equivalent is a clarification step between sender and receiver agents."
- **Qian et al. "UserBench: An Interactive Gym Environment for User-Centric Agents." arXiv:2507.22034.** Simulated users start with underspecified goals and reveal preferences incrementally. Models fully align with all intents only 20% of the time and uncover fewer than 30% of preferences. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22034)
  - **Relation:** borrowable (an incremental-reveal simulator). **Pos.:** "UserBench's simulated users reveal preferences only when asked. L4's simulated principal knows the unit and reveals it only when the question reaches it."
- **Shao, Samuel, Jiang, Yang, Yang. "Collaborative Gym." ICLR 2026; arXiv:2412.15701.** Non-turn-taking human–agent collaboration with a user simulator and a real-user condition. Collaborative agents beat autonomous ones (win rates 86% travel, 74% tabular, 66% related work). Communication failures appear in 65% of real-condition cases. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15701)
  - **Relation:** supports (communication failures dominate). **Pos.:** "Co-Gym shows that communication failures dominate human–agent collaboration. L4 isolates one such failure between agents."
- **Zhao, Fang, Cheng. ### Inferences
- Simulated-user designs fall into four families: (i) an oracle that reveals the full spec on any ask (Matsnev 2606.19559; Edwards & Schuster with a 3-turn cap; ClarifyCodeBench answers from annotations); (ii) a privileged LM that answers in role (CLAM, MediQ, τ-bench, STaR-GATE, UserBench); (iii) a *gated* or strict simulator that answers only specific, relevant questions (ToolEvaluator similarity threshold; Deng et al. strict mode; AskBench rubric-gated reveal); (iv) real users (ClarifyGPT, CollabLLM, Co-Gym). Option (iii) best addresses the reviewers' complaint, because it stops vague "can you clarify?" from counting as a successful ask.
- ClarifyCodeBench's result that *unit ambiguity is easy once visible* helps L4's story: the problem is detection under false specificity, not resolving the question once it is asked.

### Gaps
- I did not find a benchmark where *both* parties are LLM agents serving different principals and the clarification target is a unit or convention. The closest is iAgents (agents representing different humans; section e), which is not about clarification.
- Turn-cost weights in existing benchmarks are ad hoc: ATC, Steps, TKQR discount, ORA Gaussian, Weighted Success Rate. None is derived from user studies of how costly a question is.

## (d) Decision-theoretic and cost-sensitive clarification: expected value of asking, selective asking, calibration-based asking, and the metrics used

### Takeaway
A coherent decision-theoretic line now exists: ask iff the expected regret (or EVPI) of acting now exceeds the cost of asking. Humans follow this pattern (Tsvilodub et al., CogSci 2026). LLM work implements it with information gain (UoT, Deng et al., CIGAsk), EVPI (Rao & Daumé 2018; SAGE-Agent), conformal guarantees (KnowNo), budget constraints (PPO-Lagrangian), or cost-weighted success (Ask When It Pays). The recommended metrics are success-vs-asks Pareto curves, regret against an oracle policy, and cost-weighted success. Recent results find that *unconstrained* LLM asking is Pareto-inefficient, wasting questions on easy items and missing hard ones.

### Cited Findings
- **Tsvilodub, Mulligan, Snider, Hawkins, Franke. "Act or Clarify? Modeling Sensitivity to Uncertainty and Cost in Communication." CogSci 2026; arXiv:2602.02843.** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02843); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02843)
  - Model: ExpRegret(r*) = Σ_g P(g)·[max_r U(g,r) − U(g,r*)]; P(ask) = Logistic(τ·(ExpRegret(r*) − c)). "Uncertainty should matter most when acting incorrectly is costly."
  - Human results: high uncertainty raises clarification (β = 1.58). Higher error cost raises clarification (β = 4.47) and lowers direct action (β = −6.60). The step from no uncertainty to low uncertainty has a larger effect than low to high. The ask cost c was *not* manipulated; the authors note it "could be interpreted as the cost of asking".
  - **Relation: supports and borrowable (key).** This gives L4 a principled ask rule and a cost parameter to sweep. **Pos.:** "Under an expected-regret model (Tsvilodub et al. 2026), a receiver should ask when P(unit mismatch)·cost(wrong transfer) > cost(question). The hybrid's confidence channel supplies P(mismatch)."
- **Rao, Daumé III. "Learning to Ask Good Questions: Ranking Clarification Questions using Neural Expected Value of Perfect Information." arXiv:1805.04655 (ACL 2018 venue UNVERIFIED in this session).** "A good question is one whose expected answer will be useful." About 77K StackExchange posts with clarification questions. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.04655)
  - **Relation:** background (origin of EVPI for clarification). **Pos.:** background.
- **Ren et al. "Robots That Ask For Help: Uncertainty Alignment for Large Language Model Planners" (KnowNo). Ambiguity modes include "numeric uncertainties". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.01928)
  - **Relation: borrowable method (key).** L4 can calibrate the hybrid's confidence threshold with conformal prediction to guarantee a target silent-error rate (for example ≤ 1%) while minimizing the ask rate. KnowNo reports *help rate* against *success*. **Pos.:** "Like KnowNo, the hybrid can be tuned to a target error rate, and the quantity to report is the ask rate at that guarantee."
- **Rajput, Vogelstein. "Pareto-Dominant Clarification: Post-Training Coding LLMs via PPO-Lagrangian Budget Constraints." arXiv:2610.04089 (Oct 2, 2026; LMP Workshop at EMNLP 2026).** Treats clarification as a constrained MDP with an expected question budget, using HumanEvalComm and a GPT-4o-mini oracle. "Untuned clarification behavior is Pareto-inefficient: budget-constrained policies can simultaneously achieve higher accuracy and lower clarification rates." The accuracy–budget frontier is "log-shaped" with diminishing returns, and trained policies ask more on harder tasks. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04089)
  - **Relation:** borrowable (frontier plot). This is contemporaneous with L4 (days before ARR). **Pos.:** "Following budget-constrained clarification work, L4 plots correct-rate against ask-rate and reports whether the hybrid sits on the frontier."
- **Zhao et al.   - **Relation: borrowable (key metric) and supports the reviewers' objection.** Free asking inflates success. **Pos.:** "As Zhao et al. (2026) note for embodied agents, uncosted queries inflate success. L4 therefore reports cost-weighted success."
- **Li, Wu, Meng. "When and How Should an Agent Clarify? CIGAsk: Teaching LLMs to Clarify via Counterfactual Information Gain." EMNLP 2026 Findings; arXiv:2609.24290.** "Prompting alone is insufficient: models either ask for clarification on every query or ask vague questions." CIGAsk combines a Counterfactual Information Gain reward (gold-answer log-likelihood with and without the user response) with an Asymmetric Ambiguity Bonus for when to ask. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24290)
  - **Relation:** supports (prompted models over-ask) and borrowable (asymmetric reward: penalize asking on unambiguous items differently from not asking on ambiguous ones). **Pos.:** background. Contemporaneous.

- **Kirchhof, Kasneci, Kasneci. "Position: Uncertainty Quantification Needs Reassessment for Large-language Model Agents."   - **Relation: supports (key framing).** The hybrid is an instance of communicable underspecification uncertainty. Matsnev 2606.19559 explicitly answers this call. **Pos.:** "The hybrid realises Kirchhof et al.'s 'output uncertainty' direction between agents: the sender communicates its uncertainty about the convention in the message."
- **Oh et al. "Uncertainty Quantification in LLM Agents: Foundations, Emerging Challenges, and Opportunities." "Uncertainty Quantification for LLM Agents: A Taxonomy, an Evaluation Protocol, and an Empirical Study." arXiv:2609.07395.** "Confidence estimates from the agent's own responses do not consistently outperform a simple baseline".   - **Relation:** PropUQ-MAS is a **novelty threat (low–moderate)** for "uncertainty passed between agents", but it is an external estimator over a MAS graph, not a confidence the sender writes into the message. The hybrid has the sender state its uncertainty in the message, and L4 measures how the receiver acts on it."

### Inferences
- The minimal decision-theoretic account of L4: let p = P(unit mismatch | message), C_w = cost of a silent wrong action, C_q = cost of a question (with separate costs for an agent-to-agent turn and for one that escalates to the human principal), and C_r = residual error after asking. Asking is rational iff p·C_w > C_q + C_r. The hybrid works by making p observable to the receiver. Reporting outcomes across a grid of C_q/C_w ratios answers "asking isn't free" directly.
- The "safe = correct or asked" metric is the special case C_q = 0. Several 2026 papers (Ask When It Pays, RegretBench, PPO-Lagrangian) argue explicitly that this inflates scores.

### Gaps
- No paper found estimates real-world C_q/C_w ratios for agent-to-agent questions, as opposed to human-facing ones. L4 will have to state and sweep its assumptions.
- I found no empirical study of the cost of a question that a receiver agent escalates through the sender agent to the sender's human (a two-hop clarification).

## (e) Partner modelling and theory of mind between agents (interlocutor awareness, adaptive ToM)

### Takeaway
LLMs can recognize *who* they are talking to (interlocutor awareness, EMNLP 2025) and can benefit from matching their partner's ToM depth (A-ToM, AAAI 2026). But this work targets identity and coordination in games, not modelling a partner's *conventions*. iAgents (NeurIPS 2024) is the closest setting: agents mirror different humans under information asymmetry. None of these tests whether the receiver models the sender's unit conventions.

### Cited Findings
- **Choi, Li, Yang, Jin. "Agent-to-Agent Theory of Mind: Testing Interlocutor Awareness among Large Language Models." EMNLP 2025 (main), pp. 28895–28928, Anthology 2025.emnlp-main.1471; arXiv:2506.22957.** Formalizes *interlocutor awareness*: identifying and adapting to a dialogue partner's identity and characteristics through reasoning patterns, linguistic style, and alignment preferences. "LLMs reliably identify same-family peers and certain prominent model families, such as GPT and Claude". - **Mu, Zeng, Zhang, Shao, Chu, Guo, Jia, Wang, Hu. "Adaptive Theory of Mind for LLM-based Multi-Agent Coordination." AAAI 2026; arXiv:2603.16264.** "Misaligned ToM orders — mismatches in the depth of ToM reasoning between agents — can lead to insufficient or excessive reasoning about others." A-ToM estimates the partner's ToM order from prior interactions. Tested on a repeated matrix game, two grid-navigation tasks, and Overcooked. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16264)
  - **Relation:** contrast and borrowable (estimate the partner from interaction history). - **Li, Chong, Stepputtis, Campbell, Hughes, Lewis, Sycara. "Theory of Mind for Multi-Agent Collaboration via Large Language Models."   - **Relation:** borrowable (an explicit belief state, such as the receiver's belief about the sender's unit). **Pos.:** background.
- **Kim, Sclar, Zhou, Le Bras, Kim, Choi, Sap. "FANToM: A Benchmark for Stress-testing Machine Theory of Mind in Interactions." EMNLP 2023; arXiv:2310.15421.** Information-asymmetric conversations. - The partner-modelling papers suggest a cheap manipulation for L4: tell the receiver the sender's organisation, locale, or model family, and test whether that alone changes the ask rate (partner-aware grounding). A positive result connects L4 to the theme sub-topic "modelling partners".

### Gaps
- I found no paper on agents modelling a partner's *measurement or format conventions*.

## (f) Does explicit information (or stated confidence) in a message make the receiver ask less and act confidently on wrong information? (The "explicitness suppresses clarification" effect)

### Takeaway
I found **no direct prior demonstration** of the L4 effect, where adding explicit content to an inter-agent message lowers the receiver's clarification rate and raises confident wrong actions. There are close analogues. Epistemic certainty markers in prompts lower LLM accuracy (Zhou et al. 2023). Models are swayed by another model's prediction and explanation regardless of quality, and slightly more when it is framed as confident or authoritative (Anagnostidis & Bulian 2024). Tool agents fabricate or accept arguments rather than ask (NoisyToolBench). In humans, lowering *perceived* uncertainty reduces clarification (Tsvilodub et al. 2026). There is also a **contrast**: when the explicit content is a *checkable* false claim, frontier models correct it more readily than they ask about missing information (AskBench). This suggests the L4 effect is specific to information the receiver *cannot verify*, such as conventions.

### Cited Findings
- **Zhou, Jurafsky, Hashimoto. "Navigating the Grey Area: How Expressions of Uncertainty and Overconfidence Affect Language Models." EMNLP 2023 (Oral); arXiv:2302.13439.** Injecting 50 epistemic markers into QA prompts changes accuracy by more than 80%. "Expressions of high certainty result in a 7% decrease in accuracy as compared to low certainty expressions." Factive verbs hurt and evidentials help. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13439)
  - **Relation: supports (closest analogue).** Certainty framing in the *input* degrades the receiving model's behavior. **Pos.:** "L4's finding extends Zhou et al.'s epistemic-marker effect from QA to agent relays: a confident, explicit message suppresses the receiver's checking."
- **Anagnostidis, Bulian. "How Susceptible are LLMs to Influence in Prompts?" arXiv:2408.11865.** When the prompt includes another model's prediction and explanation, models "are strongly influenced, and when explanations are provided they are swayed irrespective of the quality of the explanation. The models are more likely to be swayed if the input is presented as being authoritative or confident, but the effect is small in size." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.11865)
  - **Relation: supports (direct analogue for agent-to-agent input).** **Pos.:** "Receivers defer to upstream models' stated content (Anagnostidis & Bulian 2024). L4 shows this deference extends to deciding not to ask."
- **Zhou, Hwang, Ren, Sap. "Relying on the Unreliable: The Impact of Language Models' Reluctance to Express Uncertainty." ACL 2024; arXiv:2401.06730.** LMs rarely express uncertainty. When prompted to, they are overconfident, with an average 47% error rate among confident responses. Human users "rely heavily on LM generations, whether or not they are marked by certainty", and preference annotators are biased against uncertain text. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.06730)
  - **Relation:** supports. A sender's self-reported confidence may be miscalibrated, and receivers (human or LLM) may ignore its marking. This warns that the hybrid's gain depends on calibration. **Pos.:** "Because verbalised confidence is often miscalibrated (Zhou et al. 2024), L4 reports the hybrid's robustness to a miscalibrated sender."
- **Zhao, Fang, Cheng, AskBench, arXiv:2602.11199 (contrast).** AskOverconfidence injects "confidently stated wrong intermediate claims" (for example, a wrong mechanism for metformin). Gemini's checkpoint coverage is **0.749 on AskOverconfidence vs 0.124 on AskMind**, which suggests "models more readily identify explicit false claims than proactively request missing information". Training for overconfidence correction raises redundant questions (Unq. 0.463 vs 0.030), a "caution–efficiency trade-off". — [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11199)
  - **Relation: contrast (important for framing).** Explicit *false facts* that can be checked against parametric knowledge get caught. An explicit *convention* (an unlabeled or mislabeled unit) cannot be checked, so explicitness lowers suspicion instead. **Pos.:** "Unlike false premises that models can check against world knowledge (AskBench), an explicit but unverifiable convention reduces asking. This is L4's explicitness effect."
- **Wang et al., NoisyToolBench, arXiv:2409.00557.** "Instructions with Errors" make up 17.3% of real noisy tool instructions, and "LLMs tend to arbitrarily generate the missed argument". — [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.00557v4)
  - **Relation:** supports (tool agents commit to arguments rather than question them).
- **Tsvilodub et al., arXiv:2602.02843.** The move from "no uncertainty" to "low uncertainty" changes clarification more than low to high. Clarification scales with *perceived* expected regret. — [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02843)
  - **Relation:** supports (mechanism). Explicit content lowers perceived uncertainty, so even a rational asker asks less, whether or not the content is correct.
- **Kuhn et al. (CLAM, 2212.07769):** models "rarely ask … and instead provide incorrect answers". **Zhang et al. (2410.13788):** models presuppose a single interpretation. **Shaikh et al. (2311.09144):** presumptive grounding, with preference tuning lowering question rates. — links above.
  - **Relation:** supports (baseline tendency to assume).
- **Edwards & Schuster (2603.26233):** when the Claude scaffold *did not* ask, resolve rate was higher (76.92% vs 65.99%). The detector abstains on easier, better-specified tasks. — [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26233)
  - **Relation:** contrast. In SWE tasks, "not asking" correlates with an *easier* task. In L4, "not asking" on explicit messages correlates with *silent errors*. L4 should state this inversion as a finding.

### Inferences
- L4 can name the effect precisely: **explicitness reduces the receiver's perceived uncertainty without reducing actual convention uncertainty, so it suppresses clarification.** It should cite Zhou et al. 2023 and Anagnostidis & Bulian 2024 as nearest analogues, and AskBench as the boundary condition (checkable claims are corrected).
- The hybrid's success follows from this: by sending *uncertainty* rather than more *content*, it raises the receiver's perceived uncertainty when it should be high.

### Gaps
- No study found manipulates *the presence of a unit label or confidence field* in an agent-to-agent message and measures ask rate together with silent-error rate. This appears novel to L4, though it is absence of evidence from a limited search.
- I did not verify human-communication literature on overspecification or the "illusion of transparency" in this session, so it is left out.

## Novelty threats

### Takeaway
The main threats concern the **receiver policy** and the **evaluation**, not the core finding. Ask-on-low-self-uncertainty thresholds (Matsnev 2606.19559), decoupled detectors (Edwards & Schuster 2603.26233), EVPI over tool arguments (SAGE-Agent 2511.08798), confidence-gated asking (MediQ, KnowNo), and regret or cost-weighted clarification metrics (RegretBench, Ask When It Pays) all predate or coincide with L4. L4's defensible novelty: (1) agent–agent relay across principals, where (2) the ambiguity is a convention invisible to the receiver; (3) the explicitness-suppresses-clarification effect; (4) a channel design in which the *sender* transmits calibrated uncertainty; and (5) if added, a two-tier cost evaluation separating agent turns from principal escalations.

### Cited Findings
- **High:** Matsnev, arXiv:2606.19559. Threshold θ = 0.5 on self-rated request uncertainty forces a clarification. It is mechanistically close to the hybrid's receiver rule. — [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19559)
- **Moderate:** Edwards & Schuster, arXiv:2603.26233 (EMNLP 2026): a multi-agent uncertainty-aware scaffold with a "calibrated information-seeking" claim. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26233). Suri et al., arXiv:2511.08798: structured uncertainty over tool parameters with an EVPI–cost trade-off. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08798). Ta et al., arXiv:2607.21143: regret-based clarification-policy evaluation, a threat only if L4 claims the cost-aware *metric* as novel. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21143)
- **Low–moderate:** Li et al. MediQ, arXiv:2406.00922 (confidence-gated asking). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00922). Ren et al. KnowNo, arXiv:2307.01928 (calibrated ask-for-help). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.01928). Liu et al. PropUQ-MAS, arXiv:2608.22130 (inter-agent uncertainty propagation, but externally estimated). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22130). Deng et al., arXiv:2606.03135 (information-gain clarifier in τ-bench). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03135)
- **Low:** Ask-before-Plan CEP (2406.12639), iAgents (2406.14928), Cemri et al. MAST FM-2.2 (2503.13657), ClarifyCodeBench "Units" type (2607.00711). These cover parts of the setting (dedicated clarifier, multi-principal agents, inter-agent clarification failure, unit ambiguity) but not the combination. — [Ask-before-Plan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12639); [iAgents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14928); [MAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657); [ClarifyCodeBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.00711)

### Inferences
- Suggested positioning paragraph: "Concurrent work trains or prompts single agents to ask when *their own* uncertainty about an underspecified request is high (Matsnev 2026; Edwards & Schuster 2026; Deng et al. 2026; Suri et al. 2025). In delegation across organisations, the uncertainty lives with the *other* agent, and the request looks fully specified. We show that explicitness suppresses asking, and that moving a calibrated uncertainty signal into the inter-agent message restores it. We evaluate under explicit question costs with a simulated principal."
- Avoid claiming novelty for "ask when confidence is low" as a receiver policy, or for "cost-aware clarification metrics" in general.

### Gaps
- I did not search OpenReview for ICLR 2027 submissions (not yet public as of Oct 2026) or for ARR October 2026 preprints posted after Oct 2.

## How the literature evaluates ask-vs-act (recommended protocol for L4)

### Takeaway
State-of-the-art practice (2025–2026) has five parts: (1) a simulated user holding privileged ground truth who answers only specific, relevant questions; (2) a well-specified control split to catch over-asking; (3) end-to-end outcome scoring; (4) explicit interaction cost, as turns, steps, redundant questions, cost-weighted success, or regret against a reference policy; and (5) success-vs-asks frontiers across budgets or thresholds. L4 should use all five and add a two-tier cost: agent-to-agent turns versus escalation to the human principal.

### Cited Findings
- Privileged-information user simulators: CLAM ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.07769)), τ-bench ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045)), MediQ ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00922)), and Edwards & Schuster with GPT-5.1 holding the full issue and a 3-turn cap ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26233)).
- Gated or strict simulators that prevent generic-question gaming: ToolEvaluator's similarity threshold, which otherwise replies "Sorry, I cannot provide additional information" ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.00557v4)); Deng et al.'s strict mode, which reveals information "only when the question is specific and relevant" ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.03135)); AskBench's rubric-consistent judge loop ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11199)).
- Over-asking controls: Matsnev's 50% underspecified split with clarification P/R/F1 ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19559)); Ambig-SWE's well-specified vs underspecified detection ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13069)); SAGE ClarifyBench's "explicit" split ([project page](https://manansuri.com/papers/sage-clarification/)).
- Cost metrics: Steps and Redundant-questions (NoisyToolBench, [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.00557v4)); ATC and KQC (ClarEval, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00187)); TKQR (turn-discounted) and ORA (optimal-round Gaussian) (ClarifyCodeBench, [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.00711)); Weighted Success Rate (Ask When It Pays, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03175)); regret vs reference policy plus interaction cost (RegretBench, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21143)); total interaction steps and clarification turns (Deng et al., [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.03135)); wasted compute (Gulati et al., [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07937)); user time spent (CollabLLM, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00640)).
- Frontier and budget evaluations: a fixed 10% ask budget (Zhang & Choi, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09469)); the accuracy–clarification Pareto frontier under budgets (Rajput & Vogelstein, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04089)); help rate at a guaranteed success level (KnowNo, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.01928)); threshold sensitivity at θ ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 0.75} (Matsnev, [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19559)).
- Reliability: pass^k (τ-bench, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045)).
- Decision rule and cost parameter: P(ask) = Logistic(τ·(ExpRegret − c)) (Tsvilodub et al., [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02843)).

### Inferences
Recommended L4 protocol (synthesis):
1. **Simulated principal (user) behind the sender.** An LLM given privileged ground truth: the true value, the true unit, and any other slot. It answers **only targeted questions**: "is 300,000 in USD or thousands?" gets an answer; "can you clarify?" gets "please be specific". Add stochastic principal behaviors (an "I don't know" rate, slow replies) to test robustness, following RegretBench's "robustness to user behaviors". Questions from the receiver go to the sender agent first. The sender answers from its context if it can and escalates to the principal only if needed. **Log the two hops separately.**
2. **Outcome taxonomy per episode.** (a) correct without asking; (b) silently wrong (acted, wrong unit); (c) asked → correct; (d) asked → still wrong (bad question or bad use of the answer); (e) unnecessary ask on a well-specified or matching-unit control; (f) gave up or timed out. Report all six, not "safe".
3. **Well-specified control split.** Include items where the units already match or the unit is explicit and correct, so that over-asking appears as a false-positive clarification rate. Report clarification P/R/F1 against a ground-truth "needs clarification" label, as Matsnev and Ambig-SWE do.
4. **Cost-weighted utility.** U = 1[correct] − λ_a·(#agent–agent questions) − λ_p·(#principal escalations) − λ_w·1[silently wrong]. Report curves over λ_a/λ_w and λ_p/λ_w on a log grid (for example 0.01–1), and mark where the hybrid beats always-act and always-ask. Also report Weighted Success Rate (Ask When It Pays) and **regret** against the oracle policy "ask iff the unit is actually missing or mismatched" (RegretBench).
5. **Frontier plot.** Correct-rate (and silent-error rate) against ask-rate, sweeping the hybrid's confidence threshold. Include always-ask, never-ask, and receiver-self-uncertainty baselines (the Matsnev-style threshold) as reference points. Optionally calibrate the threshold conformally (KnowNo) to a target silent-error rate such as 1%, and report the ask rate needed.
6. **Turn-aware scores.** Mean turns to resolution (ATC) and total steps. A TKQR-style discount if multiple slots can be ambiguous.
7. **Reliability.** pass^k over repeated runs (τ-bench).
8. **Conditional analysis.** Accuracy given asked vs not asked, split by explicitness condition, to test the explicitness inversion described in section (f) against Edwards & Schuster.

### Gaps
- There is no standard empirical value for λ, so it must be swept and stated. No human-study calibration of agent-to-agent question cost was found.

## Experiment ideas borrowed from this literature

### Takeaway
Eight experiments are cheap to add and map directly onto reviewer requests and theme sub-topics (partners, common ground, efficiency and reliability).

### Cited Findings
- **Strict, gated simulated principal:** from ToolEvaluator ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.00557v4)) and Deng et al.'s strict mode ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.03135)).
- **Cost sweep and regret:** from Tsvilodub et al. ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02843)), RegretBench ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21143)), and Ask When It Pays ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03175)).
- **Budget or threshold frontier:** from Zhang & Choi ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09469)), Rajput & Vogelstein ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04089)), Matsnev ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19559)), and KnowNo ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.01928)).
- **Epistemic-marker manipulation:** from Zhou et al. 2023 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13439)) and Anagnostidis & Bulian 2024 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.11865)).
- **Timing manipulation:** from Gulati et al. ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07937)).
- **Partner-identity manipulation:** from interlocutor awareness ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.22957)) and A-ToM ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16264)).
- **Consistency-based detection baseline:** from ClarifyGPT ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10996)).
- **Grounding-act coding of transcripts:** from Shaikh et al. 2024 and 2025 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09144); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13975)).

### Inferences
1. **End-to-end with a strict simulated principal (directly answers the reviewers).** Run baseline, unit-explicit, and hybrid channels. The principal answers only targeted questions, and the sender agent mediates. Report the six-way outcome taxonomy plus cost-weighted utility curves. Expected result: the hybrid dominates when λ_w/λ_a is large (wrong transfers cost much more than questions), and the baseline's 85% correct is preferred only when questions are expensive relative to silent errors. The crossover λ is the headline.
2. **Explicitness × confidence factorial (formalises L4's key finding).** Vary the unit label (absent / correct / wrong), the confidence field (absent / high / low), and epistemic markers in prose ("I'm sure it's…" vs "I think…", per Zhou et al.). Measure ask rate and silent-error rate. Prediction: a wrong-but-explicit label paired with high confidence gives the lowest ask rate and the highest silent-error rate.
3. **Miscalibrated-sender robustness.** Make the sender's confidence noisy, inverted, or always high (motivated by Zhou et al. 2024 and Lin et al. 2026). Measure how much the hybrid's 1% silent-error rate degrades. This tests whether receivers merely defer to the confidence field (cf. Anagnostidis & Bulian).
4. **Receiver-self-uncertainty baseline (pre-empts Review 3).** Implement Matsnev's prompt (u ∈ {0, 0.5, 1}, ask if u ≥ θ) on the receiver *without* sender confidence. If it under-asks on bare numbers (it should, because the request looks fully specified), that shows sender-side uncertainty is what matters.
5. **Conformal threshold calibration (KnowNo-style).** On a calibration split, choose the confidence threshold that guarantees a silent-error rate ≤ ε, then report the ask rate on test. Frame the result as "the ask rate needed to reach 1% silent errors".
6. **Two-hop cost accounting.** Separate receiver→sender questions the sender answers from context from those escalated to the principal. Report each, since agent turns are cheap and human interrupts are expensive. This exercises the theme's "efficiency & reliability" sub-topic.
7. **Partner-awareness manipulation.** Tell the receiver the sender's organisation, locale, or model family, or give a few prior interactions (A-ToM style). Test whether the receiver infers the convention or asks more when the partner is "foreign". This connects to the "modelling partners" sub-topic.
8. **Grounding-act annotation of transcripts.** Code receiver turns for clarification, acknowledgment, and repeat-back ("confirming 300,000 thousand KRW?") using Shaikh et al.'s act set. Report how often each condition elicits grounding acts. A "confirm-back" variant, where the receiver restates value and unit before acting, is a cheap grounding-act intervention worth testing against asking.

### Gaps
- None of these designs has been validated with real humans in the L4 setting. If budget allows, a small human-principal check (cf. CollabLLM's 201-judge study, Co-Gym's real condition) would test whether the simulated principal's answers are realistic.
